# Storytelling & Copywriting Toolkit

The voice files tell you how to *sound*. This file gives you the craft that makes copy actually land: how to open, how to build, how to make an abstract claim concrete, how to keep someone reading. These are tools to reach for, not a checklist to complete. A short LinkedIn post might use one. A blog article might use four. Pick what the piece needs.

Everything here bends to the voice rules. If a technique below would push you toward a banned construction (a negative comparison, a rule of three, a hedge), the voice rule wins.

## Start where the reader already is

The most reliable way to make copy feel written *for someone* is Robert Collier's principle: **enter the conversation already going on in the reader's head.** Don't open on your product or your studio. Open on the thought the reader is already having.

A CMO isn't thinking "I need a design agency." They're thinking "our competitor just rebranded and we look dated." Start there. A founder isn't thinking "I want UX work." They're thinking "users keep dropping off at onboarding and I don't know why." Start there. The Properly service-line copy already does this ("Your website is your pitch before the pitch") — the reader recognises their own situation in the first line.

## Hooks that earn the next line

The first line has one job: make the second line unavoidable. Reach for one of these, and make it true.

- **The specific-detail open.** A concrete, slightly surprising fact. "$9.7B, three years, same team, from zero." "Ninety-four percent of claimed tokens got staked, not sold." The number or detail signals real work behind the claim and pulls the reader in. The strongest hook for finance work, where specificity reads as credibility.
- **The contrarian claim.** A sharp position most people in the room would flinch at, immediately backed with substance. "Most rebrands solve the wrong problem." "The dirty secret most UX teams won't admit." Only use it if the body actually delivers the argument. A contrarian hook with nothing behind it is the worst kind of bait.
- **The real question.** A question the reader is genuinely asking themselves. "Why do 60% of fintech users drop off during KYC?" Works best as an article title or thread opener.
- **The moment.** Drop the reader into a specific scene. "A CMO lands on your homepage. Before they read a word, they've decided whether to trust you." Puts them inside the stakes.

Avoid the hooks that announce themselves as hooks: "Let's talk about…", "Here's the thing about…", "In today's fast-moving world…". They promise a payoff and deliver a throat-clear.

## Frameworks for the shape of a piece

Use these to structure the argument. They are spines, not templates. You still write every line in the voice.

- **PAS — Problem, Agitate, Solve.** Name the pain the reader feels. Make it vivid enough that they feel the cost of it. Then show the way out. Best for short conversion copy and LinkedIn posts. The Properly Product Design blurb is PAS: "Your product works" (setup) → "every drop-off, every support ticket… is revenue you're leaving on the table" (agitate) → "we design products users understand without a tutorial" (solve).
- **BAB — Before, After, Bridge.** Paint the current stuck-state, paint the better future-state, then position the work as the bridge between them. Ideal for case studies and results posts. "Spark had $0 and a blank page. Three years later, $9.7B TVL. Here's the bridge."
- **StoryBrand — the reader is the hero, you are the guide.** The client is Luke; you're Yoda. They have the ambition and the stakes; you have the map and the experience. Never cast the studio as the hero of the story. This is why Rule 2 in the guide ("talk about the client, not yourself") works: the reader wants to see themselves win, with you helping.
- **The narrative arc.** The same structure Properly designs into UX flows. Setup → rising tension → a genuine pivot or turning point → resolution → what it opened up. Use it for anything told as a journey. The pivot in the middle is what keeps it from being a straight problem-to-solution march.

## Make the abstract concrete

This is the single highest-leverage move in the whole file, and the one that most separates human copy from generated copy.

**Show, don't tell.** Ogilvy's line for the Rolls-Royce wasn't "remarkably quiet." It was "At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise comes from the electric clock." Don't say a flow is confusing. Show the user staring at a screen thinking "wait, which account is this coming from?" Don't say design builds trust. Show the investor who formed an opinion before reading a word.

**The "so what?" ladder.** Push every feature down to the payoff that actually matters. A feature ("we build design systems") → what it does ("one visual language across every screen") → the functional benefit ("your dev team stops guessing") → the business impact ("features ship without being redesigned twice") → the thing the reader feels ("you stop paying for the same work twice"). Stop at the rung that lands hardest for the specific reader. For a founder, that's usually the money. For a builder, the dev-handoff.

**Reach for the true specific over the safe general.** "Axi" over "a global broker." "Seven months" over "quickly." "94% of claimed tokens staked rather than sold" over "strong engagement." Specificity is what makes a claim sound like it came from someone who was actually there. (Numbers for Properly copy come only from §5 of the guide. If the specific figure you want isn't there, find a different true specific, or write around it. Never estimate.)

## Keep them reading

- **Open loops.** Raise a question early and hold the answer. "So I sat down and asked the developers what they actually needed. Their answer surprised me." The reader stays for the payoff. Iron rule: **close every loop you open.** An unresolved loop reads as a bait-and-switch.
- **Connective tissue between beats.** Short transitional phrases carry the reader from one thought to the next: "So I tried something." "Here's where it got interesting." "Turns out…" "And that's when it clicked." Used sparingly they create momentum. Overused they become a tic, so lean on them only at real turns, not every paragraph.
- **One idea per paragraph.** Especially on LinkedIn, where line breaks do the pacing. If a paragraph is carrying two thoughts, split it. Give each idea room to land before the next arrives.

## Rhythm

Copy has a sound, and even rhythm is the most audible AI tell there is. Machines cluster every sentence at a similar length. Humans swing.

Put a four-word sentence next to a thirty-word one. Let one thought run long and breathe, then stop it dead with a short one. Read the draft out loud. If you find yourself settling into a metronome, break it deliberately. A fragment is allowed. Starting a sentence with "And" or "But" is allowed. The goal is prose that sounds like a person talking, with the natural unevenness that comes with it.

This pairs directly with the guide's Rule 4 ("short sentences, longer thoughts") and its "vary sentence length dramatically" note. Rhythm is where voice and craft meet.
